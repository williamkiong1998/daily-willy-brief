AI’s competitive map is getting taller, not merely smarter. The company that controls silicon, cloud, model and agent can tune the joins between them—but it also inherits the responsibility for every join that breaks.

The Lead

The AI stack is becoming the product

Alibaba used its Apsara conference to lay out a single AI program spanning proprietary chips, cloud infrastructure, Qwen models, an agentic cloud and a mobile-agent platform. The company says Qwen 4 is in training and could scale to 10 trillion parameters; that is an announced ambition, not an independently verified capability. The more consequential fact is architectural. Owning more layers lets a provider optimise cost, latency, security and distribution as one system rather than a chain of vendor handoffs. It also concentrates the blame when an agent misuses a tool, the model leaks context or capacity becomes scarce. This is an operating-model bet disguised as a launch calendar.

Why it matters: products built on AI will increasingly compete on their seams—how identity, data, inference and action fit together—not on a model name alone.
The Takeaway
Vertical integration makes the system easier to tune—and harder to excuse. The winning platform will not just offer a powerful agent; it will make the agent’s compute, data boundary, authority and recovery path legible enough for an operator to trust.

Treat that as vendor research, not a market census; the design lesson survives: permissions should be evaluated where the action happens, not only when a connector is installed.

The meaningful shift is not a new badge of safety; it is making evidence work alongside development, where decisions are made.

🧠 Builder’s Notebook
Progressive authority: make the agent earn more power
Intuition: progressive authority means an agent begins with the least power needed for a useful first move, then earns broader scope through evidence and explicit approval. Think of a junior chef: they may prep ingredients freely, plate with supervision, and only later run the pass. The restaurant does not start by handing them the keys and the dinner service.

Mechanism:
request → risk classification → minimum permission → observed outcome → approval or expanded scope
Alibaba’s full-stack bet makes this especially important. A smooth stack can hide a dangerous jump from “read this record” to “change that customer’s account.” Separate data access, action authority and spend budget. Log the evidence that justified each escalation, and expire it when the task ends. That turns convenience into a reviewable contract rather than a permanent privilege.

Try this (20 min): choose one agent workflow that touches a real system. Make a three-row authority ladder: read, propose, execute. For each row, name the allowed tools, dollar or data boundary, approval trigger and expiry. If “execute” lacks a separate trigger, you have a shortcut masquerading as autonomy.
🔧 Product Craft
Design the authority ladder, not a single “connect” button
DigitalOcean’s managed-agent pitch treats isolation and brokered credentials as first-class infrastructure. That deserves a matching product surface. Do not ask a person to “connect CRM” and leave them to infer every future consequence. Start with a visible purpose: read account history to draft a renewal note. Then show the next power separately: create the note, send it, update the record. Each step should say what changes, who can undo it and what evidence will remain. The pattern feels slower only in a demo; in production, it reduces the cognitive debt that arrives when an agent’s apparently simple connection begins acting across systems.
