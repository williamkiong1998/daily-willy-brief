☕ Cold Open
A breach is usually described as a failure of a wall. The more revealing question is what still works after someone gets through it: identity, data and authority should each have a smaller room in which to misbehave.
The Lead

The breach is the boundary test

Reuters and CBS News report that hackers breached the US Air Force's Cloud One platform; an ODNI official said there were no disruptions to critical capabilities or classified systems. The public facts remain thin: the service has not described the attack path, scope or data exposure. That uncertainty is the lesson, not an invitation to speculate. A cloud estate can be compromised without becoming a single catastrophe only when its workloads, credentials and high-value data are already separated before the incident. For product teams, “secure” is too blunt a status. Ask instead which user, tool and dataset would still be reachable if one service identity were lost. Why it matters: a credible agent or cloud product needs a designed failure radius, not a reassuring perimeter story.
THE TAKEAWAY
The useful security metric is not “nothing got in”; it is “one compromised thing could do only its own small amount of damage.” That is a product property: customers experience it as continuity, recoverability, fewer emergency permissions and fewer invisible side effects.

🧠 Builder’s Notebook
Blast-radius engineering: make the bad day smaller
Intuition: blast radius is the size of the room a failure is allowed to ruin. A ship’s watertight doors are the right analogy: the hull may be damaged, but the whole vessel should not fill with water because one compartment did.

Mechanism:
compromised identityscoped credentials + segmented datacontainment + revocationrecoverable service
Application: an agent should not inherit a founder’s browser session, a broad database role and a production deployment key because all three are convenient. Give each task a narrow, expiring capability; log its use; make revocation immediate. This also improves product design because approval becomes a named object, not a nervous moment in chat. Try this (20 min): draw one agent workflow and mark every credential or data store it can touch. For each, write the smallest scope, expiry and kill switch. The broadest box is your next engineering ticket.
