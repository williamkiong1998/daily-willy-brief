The Lead
The US ends its five-day pause on Iran with a “heavy wave” of strikes
CENTCOM said it had “successfully completed a heavy wave of strikes against Iran” by 10pm ET Wednesday, hitting military command centres, missile and drone factories, and a coastal surveillance site — the reprisal for Tuesday's failed Iranian missile attack on a US base in Jordan, as covered Wednesday. Overnight strikes killed a family of three, including a two-year-old, on Qeshm Island, Iranian state media said, and Tehran vowed Thursday to “punish the aggressor.” Hours later, the Senate killed a Democrat-backed Iran War Powers resolution 49-50, while Saudi Arabia said 14 countries now back a new maritime alliance to protect Gulf shipping routes.

Why it matters: Washington answered a failed missile attack with a “heavy wave,” not proportionate restraint — whether that gap resets to a new pause or reads as fresh escalation is the next 48 hours' question.
The Takeaway
A pause that ends this fast was never a ceasefire — it was a waiting game for whichever side moved first to wear the blame, and Tuesday's failed Iranian missile handed Washington exactly that pretext. The real question isn't whether the war resumes; it's whether Thursday's “heavy wave” settles into a new lull or convinces Tehran it's now free to answer in kind.

🤖 AI & Tech
Meituan ships a trillion-parameter coding model with zero Nvidia chips, and Google DeepMind teaches humanoids to finish what they start
Meituan open-sourced LongCat-2.0, a 1.6-trillion-parameter model built for agentic coding — trained end-to-end on domestic Chinese chips with no Nvidia hardware in the loop. It already topped OpenRouter's rankings under the code name “Owl Alpha,” natively handles a 1-million-token context window, and ships under an MIT licence anyone can fold into a commercial product. The bigger story than the benchmark: a frontier-class model that never touched a GPU export ban.

Google DeepMind shipped Gemini Robotics 2 as three models in one push: a vision-language-action model for whole-body humanoid control, an embodied-reasoning model for multi-step planning, and an on-device variant that adapts to new robot hardware within hours. The headline trick is temporal intelligence — robots that track their own progress and know when a task is actually finished, not just attempted.

Nscale agreed to buy Anyscale, steward of the open-source Ray framework, for $1.65 billion — its latest move to own more of the AI compute stack after building out energy, data centres and orchestration software. Pairing Nscale's infrastructure with Ray's distributed-training orchestration is a bet that whoever controls scheduling controls the margin, not just whoever owns the GPUs.

74% of enterprises expect to run agentic AI within two years, per Deloitte's 2026 State of AI in the Enterprise — but only a small minority have built governance mature enough to manage autonomous agents at scale. The gap isn't ambition, it's oversight: adoption is outrunning anyone's ability to say what happens when an agent gets it wrong.

Brussels formally opened its call for AI Gigafactory proposals Wednesday, backing €10 billion in public funding and targeting €30 billion once private capital joins in. It's Europe's clearest signal yet that it doesn't intend to keep renting its AI infrastructure from Washington or Beijing, even if the funding gap versus US hyperscaler capex remains enormous.
🌏 World & Malaysia
A Russian missile spills into Poland, Kumamoto's death toll passes 30, and Negeri Sembilan's campaign closes with police on standby
Russia fired more than 70 missiles and 280 drones at Ukraine overnight, killing at least 10 people, and one missile crossed into Polish airspace and detonated — prompting Poland to scramble fighter jets. A residential building near Zelensky's hometown of Kryvyi Rih took a direct hit, killing several family members.

Japan's death toll from Tuesday's magnitude-7.1 Kumamoto earthquake climbed to 34, with seven killed when a shopping mall collapsed and exploded in Kashima and eight more at a paper mill in Yatsushiro. Rescuers say hope for further survivors is fading as the search stretches on.

The World Food Programme appealed for $293.6 million, warning hunger is now undermining the response to the fastest-growing Ebola outbreak on record — over 3,200 cases and 1,000 deaths in eastern DRC in just over two months. “Ebola feeds on delay, fear and hunger,” says WFP's Carl Skau.

Bursa Malaysia shrugged off Wall Street's Fed-driven wobble for a second day: the KLCI added 0.28% to 1,720.40 on defensive buying Thursday, while the ringgit held flat against the dollar as traders looked past the noise toward Friday's Big Tech earnings.

Negeri Sembilan's campaign closed Thursday with 318 Johor police mobilised to help secure Saturday's vote — a 103-candidate, three-way contest across 36 seats that could produce Malaysia's first hung state assembly since Umno's April defection collapsed the government.

Malaysia's economy grew 5.6% in the first half of 2026, up from 4.5% a year earlier, as Putrajaya frames 2026 as a year of “execution” on the 13th Malaysia Plan. Inflation cooled to 1.9% in June — rare good news heading into a contested state election.
📈 Markets
Microsoft's record day erases the Fed rout, and Apple's earnings beat still costs it 4%
The S&P 500 closed Thursday at 7,437.63 (+1.7%) and the Nasdaq Composite jumped 2.8% to 25,122.18, snapping a six-day losing streak, while the Dow added 1.2% to 52,208.06 — a full reversal of Wednesday's Fed-driven rout. The mover: Microsoft surged 16.4% on an Azure beat that crossed $100 billion in annual revenue for the first time, adding roughly $485 billion in market value in a single session — a record, eclipsing Nvidia's $441 billion gain from April 2025. Amazon jumped as much as 10% after-hours as AWS revenue grew 37%, its fastest pace in nearly five years, even as it lifted its 2026 capex outlook to $220 billion. Sharper number: Apple beat on revenue ($109.4 billion, +16%) but fell more than 4% post-market anyway, dragged down by weak Q4 guidance and a Services miss — Tim Cook's presumptive last earnings call as CEO ended in a selloff, not a victory lap (more below).

🚀 Business Story
Tim Cook closes his final quarter with a record beat — and a stock-punishing guidance miss
Apple's presumptive last earnings call under Tim Cook delivered the numbers a swan song should: revenue of $109.4 billion, up 16%, what Cook called the “strongest June quarter ever” across every product line and region. The stock fell more than 4% anyway. Component shortages pushed Q4 guidance to 9–11% growth against a 12%+ consensus, Services missed by half a billion dollars, and Greater China came in soft — exactly the spots incoming CEO John Ternus inherits when he takes over on September 1. The lesson for anyone running a company on a growth multiple: markets don't reward the quarter you just had, they price the one you're walking into — a “strongest ever” framing buys no cover for a shakier forecast. Ternus takes the job with cleaner numbers than Thursday's stock reaction suggests, but zero room to fumble the handoff.
