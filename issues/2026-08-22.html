The Lead
Malaysia may buy the company behind its identity documents
The government has asked NexG for an indicative price for Datasonic, its MyKad and passport-supply business. NexG's preliminary internal valuation is RM7.5 billion, but it stressed that talks are exploratory and no decision has been made. A buyout would move a vendor operating critical identity infrastructure closer to direct state control; it would also move delivery risk, talent retention, upgrades and cybersecurity accountability with it.

Why it matters: identity documents are not ordinary procurement. They are a trust root for banking, travel and public services, so ownership determines who controls the roadmap—and who absorbs failure.
The Takeaway
“Build versus buy” is incomplete for sovereign systems. The sharper question is which capabilities must be owned, which can be operated by a supplier, and which interfaces make that supplier replaceable without breaking public trust.
What to watch: the transaction perimeter matters more than the headline valuation. Does a deal include source code, manufacturing capacity, cryptographic key custody, staff and supplier contracts—or mainly corporate ownership? Then inspect the operating model: procurement rules may improve public accountability but slow specialist hiring and release cycles. Finally, ask how success will be measured. Unit cost and delivery time are visible; fraud prevented, recovery speed and graceful vendor exit are harder but more important. In infrastructure acquisitions, owning the legal entity and owning the system's scarce operational knowledge are not necessarily the same thing. Until those details emerge, RM7.5 billion is a negotiating anchor, not an answer to whether the state will gain control of the capabilities that actually make the system trustworthy.

🧠 Builder's Notebook
A digital identity is a trust root, not a card
Intuition: a trust root is the fact a system accepts without asking another system to prove it. Like the master key register in a hotel, every downstream check inherits confidence—or compromise—from it.

Mechanism:
issuer authority → signed credential → verifier rules → access decision
Ownership matters, but the control plane matters more: who can issue, revoke, rotate keys, change verification policy and audit anomalies? Government ownership can align accountability with sovereignty; a specialised operator may ship and secure faster. Clean interfaces, key custody and exit plans determine whether either model is resilient.

Try this (20 min): sketch your company's login flow. Mark the identity provider, token issuer, key owner, revocation path and audit log. Then ask: if one vendor disappeared tomorrow, which user journeys stop?
