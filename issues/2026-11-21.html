☕ Cold Open
The AI boom just met the part of the spreadsheet where growth has to justify its capital. The more interesting question is not whether the technology works, but which costs, contracts and interfaces will still look sensible when money is no longer free to applaud them.
The Lead

AI’s first cost-of-capital test

Friday’s AI-stock selloff was no longer a wobble in one ticker: by mid-afternoon, the S&P 500 was down 2.8% and the Nasdaq 4.2%. AMD and Broadcom were each down more than 12%, while Anthropic had fallen about 23% from its brief post-IPO high. Those are reported intraday moves, not final closes. The proximate worry is familiar—rich valuations and enormous data-centre spending—but the change is more useful to name precisely: capital is starting to demand a visible route from GPU outlay to durable cash flow. That pressure reaches beyond public markets. It changes procurement conversations, startup runway math and the tolerance for agent features with charming demos but unpriced operating costs. Why it matters: “AI strategy” is becoming a unit-economics and interface-governance problem at the same time.
THE TAKEAWAY
The builders with the best story may not win the next phase; the ones with the clearest cost boundary might. Treat model choice, approval friction and data-centre commitments as product architecture—because investors increasingly will.
🤖 AI & Tech
01  OpenAI’s updated usage policies took effect Friday, adding clearer rules for model-training use, high-stakes decisions and model-access intermediaries. A policy page is not a product launch, but it points at a practical design boundary: an app that merely uses a model is different from one that brokers direct model access for others. If you build the latter, “who is the customer?” becomes a safety and compliance question, not just a billing one.
02  Google says a Gemini-powered tool helps the nonprofit Watch Duty surface wildfire dispatches from noisy radio in under 30 seconds. That is Google’s account of a six-month fellowship using more than 20,000 hours of recordings. The product lesson is stronger than the claim: high-stakes AI earns trust by narrowing a messy input, preserving a human decision point and making the resulting alert operationally useful.
03  OpenAI’s new background-completion windows offer 30% lower token rates for two hours and 40% for eight hours, in public beta. The announcement is a vendor claim, but the pattern is durable: latency is now a pricing lever. A product team should make the “wait longer, pay less” trade explicit in the interface rather than burying it in an infrastructure setting.

🧠 Builder’s Notebook
Event protocols: contracts for work that takes time
Intuition: an event protocol is the shared grammar for an agent and its interface while work is happening. A normal API call is a restaurant order: request in, plate out. An agent run is an open kitchen: it streams, asks for a tool, pauses for approval and may delegate. If the client only understands “loading” and “done,” it cannot safely show what matters.

Mechanism:
user intent → typed run/tool/approval events → client state and controls → inspectable outcome
Application: this is why AG-UI is more than a streaming trick. A typed approval event can carry the scope, state and actor a product needs to prevent a vague “continue?” from becoming authority. It also makes cost visible: a client can show a tool call, a queued background job or a failed subagent instead of faking certainty. Try this (20 min): pick one agent flow you use. Write five event names—run started, evidence found, approval requested, action executed, run finished—and state the minimum data each must contain. You will quickly find the boundary your UI currently leaves to a prompt.
